## the wrists

I spend a minimum of 8 hours a day using a computer. You can probably imagine how many words i must be typing in a day. this means my hands are stuck in this awkward position for hours, every single day.

At some point my wrists started complaining louder than my brain. I wanted to type faster, but I also wanted to stop doing the weird wrist yoga that normal staggered keyboards force you into.

So I started looking into keyboard layouts, split keyboards, layers, and eventually a cursed little idea called home row mods.

## drumroll please "home row mods"

The home row is the row where your fingers are supposed to rest. The little bumps on `f` and `j` are homing bumps, which help your index fingers find their way back without looking down.

Home row mods take that idea and make it unhinged in a useful way.

Instead of reaching for `ctrl`, `shift`, `alt`, or `cmd`, those modifiers live under your home row keys. Tap the key and it acts like a normal letter. Hold the key and it acts like a modifier.

For example:

- tap `a` and you get `a`
- hold `a` and it becomes a modifier
- tap `s` and you get `s`
- hold `s` and it becomes another modifier

The point is not to make the keyboard more complicated. The point is to stop leaving the home row every three seconds.

## the setup

I did this with Kanata, which is a keyboard remapper that lets you define tap-hold behavior and layers without needing a custom keyboard firmware.

My current keyboards are very normal:

- Legion 5 Pro integrated laptop keyboard
- Redragon Kumara K552

That was part of the point. I wanted to see how far I could get without buying a split keyboard first.

## my layout

The actual settings matter less than the reasoning behind them.

The rule I used was simple: the most common modifiers should be the easiest to hold, and the keys I fat-finger the most should be moved away from dangerous places.

The timing settings are the part that make or break the whole thing. If the hold delay is too short, normal typing feels broken because every fast roll turns into a shortcut. If it is too long, shortcuts feel laggy and you start wondering why you did this to yourself.

So the layout is not just a keymap. It is a negotiation between comfort, speed, and how much nonsense your muscle memory is willing to tolerate.

## the learning curve

This was not instantly good.

Setting up Kanata was the easy part. The hard part was retraining muscle memory that had been quietly forming for years.

I practiced for about a week before it started feeling less cursed. Even now, I am still not fully used to backspace. That one key has apparently been carrying decades of emotional baggage.

## layers vs home row mods

I got the layers idea from split keyboards.

Split keyboards often use a layered key structure where you hold a modifier, usually with your thumb, and the keyboard temporarily becomes a different keyboard. Suddenly the same physical keys can become arrows, numbers, symbols, navigation, media controls, whatever.

That is different from home row mods.

Home row mods are mostly about making modifiers easier to reach. Layers are about making the whole keyboard smaller and more programmable.

The tradeoff looks something like this:

- home row mods are easier to add to a normal keyboard
- layers are more powerful once you commit to them
- home row mods can interfere with fast rolling letter patterns
- layers can feel like learning a second keyboard
- both are trying to solve the same problem: stop reaching so much

I do not think one is strictly better. Home row mods feel like a gateway drug. Layers feel like the thing you eventually build your whole keyboard around.

## was it worth it?

The short answer: probably.

The longer answer is that it depends what you are optimizing for. If you just want to type a little faster, this might be overkill. If your wrists hurt and you spend most of your life on a computer, it starts to make a lot more sense.

For me, the appeal is not just speed. It is comfort, fewer reaches, and making the keyboard feel like something I control instead of something I tolerate.

## what's next

The obvious next step is a split keyboard.

Maybe I buy one. Maybe I build one completely from scratch. Either way, home row mods made me realize the keyboard is not some fixed object. It is a programmable interface, and the default layout is just one very popular opinion.

Unfortunately, now that I know that, I may never be normal again.
